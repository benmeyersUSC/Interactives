The stock at the horizon

Present value, value weighting and the strike

Assumptions

  • The price follows geometric Brownian motion with constant drift μ and volatility σ; there are no dividends or other cash flows. All values are in units of today’s price, S₀ = 1.
  • Discounting is continuous at a constant rate r over the same horizon: every outcome is multiplied by D. Discounting moves outcomes, not probabilities, so g still integrates to 1.
  • The strike K is an amount paid at the horizon, quoted as a multiple of S₀. On the present-value axes it sits at KD, so it moves with the curves when you change r.
  • Efficient market: today’s price is the present value of the stock’s expected price at the horizon. Discounting at r, that requires μ = r. Otherwise E[Y] ≠ 1, and the check says how much more or less the stock should be worth today if its drift really were μ. The switch ties μ to r, which is the risk-neutral density that prices are weighed with.
  • With the switch on, plot 04 is the Black–Scholes formula. The value above the strike, Φ(d₁), is the number of shares that replicates the call. It is the probability of finishing in the money when outcomes are weighted by the stock’s value, because in those states you must deliver the stock. The dashed curve K·D·g(y) is the strike’s value density. Its area above the strike, K·e^(−rΔt)·Φ(d₂), is the cash you borrow: the strike’s present value times the plain probability of paying it (plot 03). The sliver between the two curves is the call. The share count Φ(d₁) changes as the price moves and time passes; at expiry it is 1 in the money and 0 otherwise.

How the plots are drawn

Plots 01, 03 and 04 share one horizontal axis, so a point in plot 01 and its discounted image in plot 03 can be compared directly. Axes change in discrete, rounded steps and only when the curves outgrow them or shrink to less than half of them, so a slider moves the curve rather than the frame. Axis changes ease in over a few frames. Each curve combines a linear grid with a logarithmic grid centred on its peak, so narrow peaks are not missed. Shading ends exactly at its threshold. Reported areas and probabilities are closed-form and include any part of a curve beyond the visible range.

The horizon slider uses log(1 + Δt / one day): short horizons get more room and zero stays reachable. A year is 365.25 days, a month 1/12 year and a quarter 1/4 year. The normal distribution function uses the Numerical Recipes complementary error function (fractional error below 1.2×10⁻⁷ everywhere, including the tails). At S = 0 every outcome equals the mean, so all four plots show point masses.
