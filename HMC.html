Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, one leapfrog step at a time

The math, built forward: from “I want samples of $\pi(q)$” to leapfrog and accept/reject

0. The objects on the table

The position $q = (q_1, q_2)$ is what we want samples of. The momentum $p = (p_1, p_2)$ is an auxiliary variable we will invent in step 2. The target is a Gaussian with mean $\mu = (\mu_1, \mu_2)$ and covariance

$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 \\[3pt] \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{1-\rho^2}\begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{1}{\sigma_1^2} & -\dfrac{\rho}{\sigma_1\sigma_2} \\[10pt] -\dfrac{\rho}{\sigma_1\sigma_2} & \dfrac{1}{\sigma_2^2} \end{pmatrix}.$$

Those five numbers, $\mu_1, \mu_2, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \rho$, are the sliders in the first control card.

1. The goal, and the potential $U$

We want samples from $\pi(q) = \mathcal N(q;\, \mu, \Sigma) \propto \exp\!\big(-\tfrac12 (q-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (q-\mu)\big)$. Name the thing in the exponent the potential energy, $U(q) = -\log \pi(q) + \text{const}$:

$$U(q) = \tfrac12 (q-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (q-\mu) = \frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\left[ \frac{(q_1-\mu_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2} \;-\; \frac{2\rho\,(q_1-\mu_1)(q_2-\mu_2)}{\sigma_1\sigma_2} \;+\; \frac{(q_2-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma_2^2} \right].$$

So $\pi(q) \propto e^{-U(q)}$: high probability is low potential, and the mode $\mu$ is the bottom of a bowl. The thin ellipses on the plot are level sets of $U$, $(q-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(q-\mu) = r^2$ for $r = 1, 2, 3$. The gradient is

$$\nabla U(q) = \Sigma^{-1}(q-\mu),$$

a vector perpendicular to the contour through $q$, pointing uphill in $U$ (away from $\mu$). The amber arrow on the plot is $-\nabla U(q)$: downhill, toward the mode. Note that with correlation the amber arrow is not parallel to the grey radial arrow; it is normal to the ellipse, which is a different direction unless $\Sigma$ is a multiple of the identity.

Why not just random-walk Metropolis? A random walk with step size $\delta$ has, after $n$ steps, travelled about $\delta\sqrt n$ (independent increments: the variances add, so the standard deviation grows like $\sqrt n$). To cross a posterior of width $w$ it needs $n \sim (w/\delta)^2$ steps, and $\delta$ cannot be large or nearly every step lands somewhere improbable and is rejected. We want a proposal that travels a long, deliberate path and still arrives somewhere probable.

2. Invent a momentum: $H = U + K$

Introduce $p \in \mathbb R^2$, the same dimension as $q$, with kinetic energy

$$K(p) = \tfrac12\, p^\top p = \tfrac12\,(p_1^2 + p_2^2)$$

(this is the choice of a unit mass matrix, $M = I$; one can use other $M$ but we will not here). Define a joint density over $(q, p)$ by

$$\pi(q, p) \propto e^{-H(q,p)}, \qquad H(q,p) = U(q) + K(p).$$

Because $H$ is a sum, the exponential factorises: $e^{-H} = e^{-U(q)}\, e^{-K(p)}$. So under the joint, $q \sim \pi$ and independently $p \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$. Two consequences. First, if we can produce samples of the joint and throw away the $p$ coordinates, what remains is a sample of $\pi(q)$. Second, the $p$ marginal is a standard Gaussian we can draw directly, which is exactly what “draw $p$” at the start of each iteration does.

3. Hamilton’s equations: a motion that leaves $\pi(q,p)$ unchanged

Treat $H$ as a physical energy and let $(q, p)$ move according to

$$\dot q = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p} = p, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q} = -\nabla U(q) = -\Sigma^{-1}(q-\mu).$$

In components, $\dot q_1 = p_1$, $\dot q_2 = p_2$, and

$$\begin{pmatrix}\dot p_1 \\ \dot p_2\end{pmatrix} = -\frac{1}{1-\rho^2}\begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{q_1-\mu_1}{\sigma_1^2} - \dfrac{\rho\,(q_2-\mu_2)}{\sigma_1\sigma_2} \\[10pt] \dfrac{q_2-\mu_2}{\sigma_2^2} - \dfrac{\rho\,(q_1-\mu_1)}{\sigma_1\sigma_2} \end{pmatrix}.$$

Two facts make this motion useful.

(a) Energy is conserved. Differentiate $H$ along the motion using the chain rule:

$$\frac{dH}{dt} = \nabla U(q)\cdot \dot q + p \cdot \dot p = \nabla U(q)\cdot p - p\cdot \nabla U(q) = 0.$$

So a trajectory lives on one shell $H = H_0$ in $(q,p)$ space, and the density $e^{-H}$ has the same value at every point along it.

(b) The flow preserves volume. The velocity field in $(q,p)$ space is $(\dot q, \dot p) = (p, -\nabla U(q))$. Its divergence is $\sum_i \partial \dot q_i / \partial q_i + \sum_i \partial \dot p_i / \partial p_i = 0 + 0$, because $\dot q$ does not depend on $q$ and $\dot p$ does not depend on $p$. A blob of points carried by the flow keeps its volume (Liouville’s theorem).

Put together: take a cloud of points distributed as $e^{-H}$ and push every one of them along the flow for the same time $T$. Each point keeps its density value by (a), and no region is compressed or stretched by (b), so the cloud is still distributed as $e^{-H}$. The exact flow is a valid, deterministic, far-travelling move that leaves the joint distribution invariant.

For the Gaussian the flow is explicit. Take $\rho = 0$ first: then $\ddot q_1 = \dot p_1 = -(q_1-\mu_1)/\sigma_1^2$, a harmonic oscillator with angular frequency $\omega_1 = 1/\sigma_1$, so

$$q_1(t) = \mu_1 + A\cos(t/\sigma_1 + \phi), \qquad \text{period } 2\pi\sigma_1.$$

Half a period, $\pi\sigma_1$, carries $q_1$ to the far side of the mode. Narrow directions oscillate fast and wide directions slowly. With $\rho \ne 0$ the same statement holds along the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$, with $\sigma$ replaced by the square root of the corresponding eigenvalue.

4. The shell, and why the fresh $p$ is (almost) tangent to it

Each iteration begins by drawing $p \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ at the current $q$. The energy is then fixed:

$$H_0 = U(q) + \tfrac12 |p|^2 .$$

Along the trajectory $K \ge 0$, so $U(q) \le H_0$ everywhere: $q$ is confined inside the dashed ellipse $U(q) = H_0$, which is $(q-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(q-\mu) = 2H_0$. Those are the turning points, where all the energy would be potential and $p$ would vanish. Drawing $p$ inflates the shell outward by exactly $K$.

Now the direction of $p$. Let $u = (q-\mu)/|q-\mu|$ be the unit radial vector (the grey dashed arrow, normalised) and $\theta$ the angle between $p$ and $u$:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{p^\top u}{|p|}.$$

The numerator $p^\top u = \sum_i p_i u_i$ is a linear combination of independent $\mathcal N(0,1)$ variables whose weights satisfy $\sum_i u_i^2 = 1$, so $p^\top u \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ exactly, in any dimension $d$. The denominator: $|p|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^d p_i^2 \sim \chi^2_d$, with mean $d$ and variance $2d$, so $|p| \approx \sqrt d$ with relative fluctuations of order $\sqrt{2/d}$. Hence

$$\cos\theta \approx \frac{\mathcal N(0,1)}{\sqrt d}.$$

In $d = 2$ the standard deviation of $\cos\theta$ is about $1/\sqrt 2 \approx 0.7$: the radial and tangential parts of $p$ are comparable, and you will see draws that point almost straight out of the bowl. In $d = 100$, $|\cos\theta| \lesssim 0.1$ nearly always: the sampled $p$ is almost surely nearly tangent to the shell, and the trajectory runs around the typical set rather than in and out of it. That is the geometry HMC exploits, and the reason it scales to high dimension. The readout shows $\cos\theta$ live; the status line reports it for each fresh draw.

The radial part of $p$ is a bounded oscillation: it climbs the bowl and comes back. The tangential part is what carries $q$ around the shell to a genuinely new place. The orbit you see is both at once.

5. Leapfrog: simulating the flow without solving it

Choose a step size $\varepsilon$. One leapfrog step takes $(q, p)$ to $(q', p')$ by three sub-moves:

$$p_{1/2} = p - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\nabla U(q) \quad\text{(half kick)},\qquad q' = q + \varepsilon\, p_{1/2} \quad\text{(drift)},\qquad p' = p_{1/2} - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\nabla U(q') \quad\text{(half kick)}.$$

Repeat $L$ times. For our Gaussian every $\nabla U$ is the matrix–vector product $\Sigma^{-1}(q-\mu)$ written out in step 3. Tick “show the kick, drift, kick” in the View card to see each sub-move: a kick changes only $p$ (the teal arrow turns by half a step of the amber one), a drift changes only $q$ (the point slides along the teal arrow).

Why this and not plain Euler? Each sub-move adds to one variable a function of the other variable only: it is a shear. A shear’s Jacobian is triangular with ones on the diagonal, so its determinant is $1$, so each sub-move preserves volume exactly, and so does their composition. Property (b) survives discretisation untouched. The scheme is also exactly reversible: negate $p$, run the same $L$ steps, negate $p$ again, and you land back where you started, because the sequence kick, drift, kick reads the same in both directions. Property (a) does not survive exactly: $H$ drifts and oscillates by an amount of order $\varepsilon^2$ (the sparkline in the side column). That residual is what accept/reject will correct.

Stability, with a derivation specific to the Gaussian. Along a direction with standard deviation $\sigma$ the dynamics are $\ddot q = -(q-\mu)/\sigma^2$, i.e. $\omega = 1/\sigma$. Substituting the three sub-moves into one another, one leapfrog step is the linear map

$$\begin{pmatrix} q' \\ p' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \dfrac{\varepsilon^2\omega^2}{2} & \varepsilon \\[10pt] -\varepsilon\omega^2 + \dfrac{\varepsilon^3\omega^4}{4} & 1 - \dfrac{\varepsilon^2\omega^2}{2} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} q \\ p \end{pmatrix}.$$

Its determinant is $1$ (volume preservation again) and its trace is $2 - \varepsilon^2\omega^2$. A $2\times2$ matrix with determinant $1$ has eigenvalues on the unit circle, meaning bounded orbits, exactly when $|\mathrm{trace}| \le 2$. That is $\varepsilon\omega \le 2$, i.e. $\varepsilon \le 2\sigma$. The narrowest direction sets the limit:

$$\varepsilon \lt 2\,\sigma_{\min}, \qquad \sigma_{\min} = \sqrt{\text{smallest eigenvalue of } \Sigma}.$$

Try it: push $\varepsilon$ past the limit printed in the integrator card. The trajectory blows up along the narrow axis, $H$ explodes, and every proposal is rejected.

6. Accept or reject

After $L$ steps we hold a proposal $(q^*, p^*)$. Accept it with probability

$$\alpha = \min\!\Big(1,\; \exp\!\big(-\,[\,H(q^*, p^*) - H(q, p)\,]\big)\Big) = \min\big(1, e^{-\Delta H}\big).$$

Why exactly this: the proposal map ($L$ leapfrog steps followed by negating $p$) is its own inverse and has Jacobian $1$, so the only thing that can break detailed balance for the joint density $e^{-H}$ is the change in $e^{-H}$ itself, and that ratio is $e^{-\Delta H}$. If the integrator were exact, $\Delta H$ would be $0$ and $\alpha = 1$ every time; in practice $\Delta H$ is small and most proposals are accepted. On accept, $q \leftarrow q^*$. On reject, $q$ stays where it was. Either way $p$ is thrown away and redrawn next iteration, so the momentum negation never has to appear in the code.

7. What gets tallied

One outer iteration yields one sample: the endpoint if accepted, the starting point if not. The intermediate leapfrog points are not samples of $\pi$. They all lie on a single energy shell $H \approx H_0$, and their $q$ values are strung along one curve. Only by redrawing $p$ (a new shell) many times do the endpoints fill in $\pi$. The histograms compare the recorded $q_1$ and $q_2$ with the exact marginals $\mathcal N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $\mathcal N(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$, read straight off the diagonal of $\Sigma$.

There is also a two-dimensional check, and it is where the light indigo contours come from. Write $r^2 = (q-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (q-\mu)$. If $q \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$ then $z = \Sigma^{-1/2}(q-\mu) \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, so $r^2 = z_1^2 + z_2^2$ is a sum of two squared independent standard normals: a $\chi^2_2$ variable, which is an exponential with mean $2$. Hence

$$P(r^2 \le c) = 1 - e^{-c/2}.$$

So the ellipse $r^2 = c$ encloses probability mass $1 - e^{-c/2}$, and the region holding mass $m$ is $r \le \sqrt{-2\ln(1-m)}$: $r = 1.18$ for $50\%$, $r = 2.15$ for $90\%$, $r = 3.03$ for $99\%$. A correct sampler must put those fractions of its samples inside those ellipses, which the moments table reports for the first two. For comparison, the grey $r = 1, 2, 3$ lines are level sets of $\pi$ at $e^{-r^2/2} = 61\%$, $14\%$ and $1.1\%$ of its peak, and they enclose $39\%$, $86\%$ and $98.9\%$ of the mass.

8. Tuning $\varepsilon$ and $L$: the U-turn

From step 3, along a direction with standard deviation $\sigma$ the flow returns to its start after time $2\pi\sigma$ and is farthest away at $\pi\sigma$. A trajectory of total length $L\varepsilon \approx \pi\sigma$ (for the widest direction) is the longest useful move; longer and the orbit turns back toward where it began, spending gradient evaluations to undo its own progress; much shorter and the sampler is diffusive again. The integrator card prints $L\varepsilon$ next to $\pi\sigma_{\max}$ and $\pi\sigma_{\min}$ so you can place yourself.

Try it: the defaults $\varepsilon = 0.15$, $L = 20$ give $L\varepsilon = 3 \approx \pi \cdot 1$ for the unit target. Stretch $\sigma_1$ to $1.5$ and watch the orbit undershoot along $q_1$; shrink $\sigma_2$ to $0.3$ and watch it over-rotate along $q_2$ while $\varepsilon$ creeps toward $2\sigma_{\min}$. Stopping before the U-turn, automatically, per trajectory, is what NUTS adds to this picture.
